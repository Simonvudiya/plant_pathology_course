San José Scale (Diaspidiotus perniciosus / Quadraspidiotus perniciosus)

▼
Note: Quadraspidiotus perniciosus is a synonym of Diaspidiotus perniciosus (also placed in genus Comstockaspis by some authors). Both names refer to the same species.
🔬 Classification
KingdomAnimalia
PhylumArthropoda
ClassInsecta
OrderHemiptera
SuborderSternorrhyncha
SuperfamilyCoccoidea
FamilyDiaspididae (armored scales)
GenusDiaspidiotus (syn. Quadraspidiotus, Comstockaspis)
Speciesperniciosus (Comstock, 1881)

Common names: San José scale, California scale, pernicious scale.

🔍 Identification
  • Adult female: Circular, slightly convex, grey scale approximately 1.5–2.2 mm in diameter, with the exuviae (cast skins) central or subcentral. Removing the scale reveals a pyriform (pear-shaped) yellow body. The pygidium (posterior end) bears two pairs of lobes (one median, one lateral) and three short, largely spaced combs. Key diagnostic feature: absence of perivulvar pores around the vulva, which distinguishes it from similar armored scales such as D. ostreaeformis, D. marani, and D. pyri.
  • Adult male: Only has forewings (hind wings reduced to halteres), no mouthparts. A characteristic dark band across the dorsum differentiates males from other Diaspididae.
  • Crawlers (first instar): Mobile, about 0.3 mm long, yellowish, oval, with three pairs of legs, antennae, and simple eyes. The scale covering is white or black.
  • Second instar nymphs: Grey cover, round to elongated depending on sex.
🧪 Protocol for Permanent Slide Preparation
The standard method for scale insect slide mounting involves the following stages:
  1. Collection and preservation: Collect live specimens and preserve in 70–75% ethanol, or in lactic acid/ethanol solution.
  2. Maceration: Transfer specimens to 10% KOH (potassium hydroxide) and heat to boiling point. Soak for one to several days at room temperature to soften and clear body contents.
  3. Bleaching (if required): For dark specimens, bleach to remove excess pigmentation.
  4. Acidification: Treat with acid to neutralize the KOH.
  5. Staining: Transfer to acid fuchsin stain for approximately 5 minutes to enhance contrast of morphological features.
  6. Dehydration: Pass through an ethanol series (50%, 70%, 95%, 100%) to remove all water.
  7. Clearing: Transfer to clove oil or xylene until specimens become transparent.
  8. Mounting: Place a small amount of Canada balsam (or Entellan®) on a microscope slide. Position the specimen, add a cover slip, and label with collection information (host plant, locality, date, collector).
  9. Drying: Place slides in a drying oven for at least two weeks to cure the mounting medium.
🌿 Signs on Affected Crops
Bark and TwigsSmall, flat, circular to oval scales appearing as a greyish crust on branches. Around each feeding site, a small reddish halo develops on the bark, extending internally to the xylem.
FruitRed to purple haloes develop around feeding sites on fruit, rendering them unmarketable.
LeavesYellowing, thinning of foliage, reduced vigor; dieback of twigs and branches during heavy outbreaks.
Severe InfestationsCrust-like covering of small, flat, round, and oval white-grey to blackish scales on bark. Can kill young trees within 2–3 years if uncontrolled.
🌱 Hosts in Dataset
Ageratum Aster Ficus Monstera Philodendron selloum Hypericum Solidago

Colorado Potato Beetle (Leptinotarsa decemlineata)

▼
🔬 Classification
KingdomAnimalia
PhylumArthropoda
ClassInsecta
OrderColeoptera
FamilyChrysomelidae
GenusLeptinotarsa
Speciesdecemlineata (Say, 1824)
🔍 Identification
  • Adult: 8.5–11.5 mm in length, round to oval-shaped, hemispherical body. Yellow-orange prothorax with black spots; elytra (wing covers) are pale yellow with five black longitudinal stripes per elytron (10 total).
  • Larva: Round and globular, brick red to orange in color, hump-backed, soft-bodied, approximately 15 mm when fully grown. Two conspicuous rows of dark spots along each side of the body.
  • Eggs: Orange-yellow, oval, laid in masses of 60 on the underside of leaves.
  • Pupae: Found in soil, smooth and shiny brown.
🧪 Protocol for Permanent Slide Preparation
For adult beetles, slide mounting of genitalia is often necessary for species-level confirmation:
  1. Detach genitalia: Remove the male aedeagus or female spermatheca using fine forceps under a stereomicroscope.
  2. Maceration: Soak in 10% KOH at room temperature for 24 hours, or heat gently until cleared.
  3. Dehydration: Pass through an ethanol series (50%, 70%, 95%, 100%).
  4. Clearing: Transfer to clove oil or xylene.
  5. Mounting: Place in Canada balsam on a slide, add cover slip, and label.
For larvae, the chaetotaxy (arrangement of setae) on the abdominal segments can be examined by mounting the larval skin after clearing and staining.
🌿 Signs on Affected Crops
DefoliationAdults and larvae feed on leaf edges, quickly stripping foliage from young plants. Can completely defoliate plants in severe cases.
ExcrementCharacteristic black and sticky excrement left on stems and leaves by larvae and adults.
Yield Loss20–100% reduction in potato yield through foliage feeding; damages aubergines and tomato by eating early fruit.
🌱 Hosts in Dataset
Irish potato (main host) Other Solanaceae

Vegetable Leafminer (Liriomyza sativae)

▼
🔬 Classification
KingdomAnimalia
PhylumArthropoda
ClassInsecta
OrderDiptera
FamilyAgromyzidae
GenusLiriomyza
Speciessativae (Blanchard, 1938)

Close relatives: L. trifolii (American serpentine leafminer) and L. huidobrensis (South American leafminer). All three are regulated quarantine pests in many regions.

🔍 Identification
  • Adult: Small fly, 1.3–2.3 mm in length, predominantly pale to bright yellow with a shiny black dorsal thorax, a yellow spot below the wings (on the scutellum), and a black and yellow striped abdomen. Key diagnostic feature: the shiny black mesonotum (upper thorax) distinguishes L. sativae from L. trifolii, which has a greyish-black mesonotum.
  • Larva: Legless maggot up to 3 mm long, changing from almost transparent to pale green then yellow or yellow-orange as it matures.
  • Pupa: Red/brown or golden brown, approximately 1.5 mm long, oval-shaped, segmented. Pupates in soil.
  • Eggs: White, oval, approximately 0.23 mm long and 0.13 mm wide, laid singly within leaf punctures.
🧪 Protocol for Permanent Slide Preparation
Species-level identification of Liriomyza requires examination of adult male genitalia:
  1. Collection: Preserve adults in 70–95% ethanol. For larvae and pupae, rear to adult emergence if possible.
  2. Dissection: Remove the male genitalia (aedeagus and associated structures) using fine micro-pins under a stereomicroscope.
  3. Maceration: Place genitalia in 10% KOH at room temperature for 12–24 hours, or heat gently until cleared.
  4. Dehydration: Pass through an ethanol series (50%, 70%, 95%, 100%).
  5. Clearing: Transfer to clove oil or xylene.
  6. Mounting: Place in Canada balsam or Hoyer's medium on a slide. Add cover slip and label.
For larvae, the cephalopharyngeal skeleton and posterior spiracles are diagnostic and can be mounted after clearing.
🌿 Signs on Affected Crops
Feeding PuncturesRounded, about 0.2 mm in diameter, appearing as white speckles on the upper leaf surface from adult feeding and oviposition.
MinesTightly coiled, pale green to white tunnels with dark thread-like frass, created by feeding larvae. Mines increase in size as larvae grow, with some areas having a dried brown appearance.
StipplingMottled yellow pattern on leaves during heavy infestations.
Secondary EffectsSun scald of fruit and flowers, lodging of stems during windy weather; entry points for secondary infections.
Yield LossCan cause up to 80% loss in tomato, particularly damaging to young crops.
🌱 Hosts in Dataset
Limonium Vegetables (Solanaceae, Fabaceae) Ornamentals

Turnip Moth / Common Cutworm (Agrotis segetum)

▼
Note: The dataset lists "Agoritis Segetum" — the correct scientific name is Agrotis segetum (Denis & Schiffermüller, 1775).
🔬 Classification
KingdomAnimalia
PhylumArthropoda
ClassInsecta
OrderLepidoptera
FamilyNoctuidae
GenusAgrotis
Speciessegetum (Denis & Schiffermüller, 1775)

Common names: Turnip moth, common cutworm, greasy cutworm, winter moth.

🔍 Identification
  • Adult: Medium-sized moth with a wingspan of 33–45 mm. Forewings are grey-brown with variable markings including a kidney-shaped (reniform) marking and a circular marking. Males have lighter forewings than females.
  • Larva: 30–45 mm when fully grown, clay-colored or grey with faint dark lines along the sides of the body. Often found curled up near the base of plants. Larvae are generally greasy in appearance (hence "greasy cutworm").
  • Pupa: Smooth, shiny brown, 10–15 mm long, found in soil inside an earthen cell made by the final instar larva.
  • Eggs: Rounded, with a brown spiral marking.
🧪 Protocol for Permanent Slide Preparation
Species-level identification of Agrotis requires examination of adult genitalia or larval chaetotaxy:
  1. Adult genitalia dissection: Remove the abdomen and macerate in 10% KOH overnight. Dissect the genitalia (male aedeagus, female bursa copulatrix) under a stereomicroscope.
  2. Larval identification: For larvae, examine the chaetotaxy (arrangement of setae) on the prothorax and abdominal segments, and the structure of the spiracles.
  3. Dehydration and clearing: Pass through ethanol series, clear in clove oil or xylene.
  4. Mounting: Mount in Canada balsam or Hoyer's medium. Label with all collection data.
Molecular identification: A real-time PCR assay targeting the mitochondrial Cytochrome Oxidase I (COI) gene has been developed for rapid identification of A. segetum at all life stages.
🌿 Signs on Affected Crops
Cutting Off SeedlingsLarvae bite through the stems of young seedlings at or just below the soil surface, causing the plant to fall over and die — the classic "cutworm" damage.
WiltingAffected plants appear wilted, similar to disease-induced wilt; removing the plant from soil reveals the damaged crown/stem base.
External FeedingLeaves, stalks, and stems show external feeding damage with abnormal leaf fall.
Root and Stem FeedingBoth external and internal feeding visible on roots and stems.
Seasonal DamageMost severe in April–May when larvae attack seedlings. Larvae emerge from burrows at night to feed.
🌱 Hosts in Dataset
Stevia rebaudiana Polyphagous (vegetables, cereals, ornamentals)

Thrips (Order Thysanoptera)

▼
🔬 Classification
KingdomAnimalia
PhylumArthropoda
ClassInsecta
OrderThysanoptera
FamiliesThripidae (common pest thrips), Phlaeothripidae

Important pest species: Thrips palmi, Frankliniella occidentalis (western flower thrips), Thrips tabaci (onion thrips).

🔍 Identification
  • Adult: Minute insects, typically 1–2 mm long, slender, with fringed wings (when present). Color varies from yellow to brown to black depending on species. The most reliable identification is based on microscopic examination of slide-mounted specimens, particularly characters such as:
    • Number and arrangement of setae on the head, pronotum, and abdomen
    • Structure of the antennae (number of segments, sensoria)
    • Presence/absence of ocelli
    • Shape of the ovipositor (in females)
    • Chaetotaxy of the forewing veins
  • Larvae: Similar to adults but wingless, often yellow or white.
🧪 Protocol for Permanent Slide Preparation
The standard protocol for thrips slide mounting involves the following steps:
  1. Collection: Collect specimens from flowers, leaves, or sticky traps. If from sticky traps, remove using glue-removing fluids based on citrus oils, dichloromethane, or a turpentine substitute.
  2. Preservation: Place specimens immediately in 10% ethanol with a drop of detergent, then transfer to 70% ethanol after one week for long-term storage. Alternatively, preserve in AGA solution (10 parts 60% ethyl alcohol : 1 part glycerin : 1 part acetic acid).
  3. Maceration: Remove specimens from storage and make a slight cut at the membranous region of the abdomen between middle segments using a fine micro-pin to remove inner body contents.
  4. Clearing: Transfer specimens through a series of solutions (e.g., 10% KOH, distilled water, 50% ethanol) to clear and soften. For dark specimens, leave longer until ommatidia become transparent.
  5. Dehydration: Pass through an ethanol series (70%, 95%, 100%).
  6. Clearing: Transfer to clove oil or xylene.
  7. Mounting: Place specimens in a drop of mounting medium (Hoyer's liquid, Canada balsam, or natural Canada balsam) on a microscope slide. Arrange legs and antennae to be visible. Add cover slip.
  8. Drying: Cure slides in an oven at 40–45°C for several days to several weeks.
  9. Labeling: Record host plant, locality, date, collector, and identifier.
🌿 Signs on Affected Crops
Silvered or Bronzed AppearanceHeavily infested leaves often have a silvery or bronzy sheen due to feeding damage and empty cells.
StipplingTiny white or yellow speckles on leaves from feeding punctures.
Leaf DistortionStunted leaves and terminals; curling or cupping of leaves.
Fruit ScarringScarred and deformed fruits; scarring around feeding sites.
Flower DamageDiscoloration, necrosis, or premature drop of flowers; thrips often hide deep within flower buds and petals.
Vector of VirusesThrips transmit tospoviruses (TSWV, INSV, etc.), which can cause additional symptoms including ringspots, necrosis, and wilting.
🌱 Hosts in Dataset
Orchid (India) Many ornamental and vegetable crops

Mealybugs (Family Pseudococcidae)

▼
🔬 Classification
KingdomAnimalia
PhylumArthropoda
ClassInsecta
OrderHemiptera
SuborderSternorrhyncha
SuperfamilyCoccoidea
FamilyPseudococcidae (mealybugs)

Common pest genera: Planococcus, Pseudococcus, Paracoccus, Phenacoccus, Maconellicoccus.

🔍 Identification
  • Adult female: Wingless, oval-shaped, up to 3 mm long, white to yellowish or light brown, covered with a white powdery wax coating. Some species have marginal waxy filaments. The body is soft and segmented; legs and antennae are often brown. Species-level identification requires slide-mounted adult females, examining features such as:
    • Number and arrangement of cerarii (waxy pore clusters)
    • Circulus (ventral abdominal structure) — presence/absence and shape
    • Number of antennal segments
    • Leg morphology (especially hind leg)
    • Ostioles (dorsal pores)
  • Adult male: Small, winged, gnat-like, short-lived, rarely seen.
  • Nymphs: Similar to adult females but smaller, with less wax.
🧪 Protocol for Permanent Slide Preparation
The standard protocol for mealybug slide mounting follows the general scale insect method with modifications for their soft bodies:
  1. Collection: Preserve specimens in 70–80% ethanol. Collect adult females preferentially, as they possess the most diagnostic characters.
  2. Maceration: Place specimens in cold 10% NaOH (sodium hydroxide) for 24 hours to soften body contents. Alternatively, use 10% KOH with gentle heating.
  3. Bleaching: For dark specimens, bleach briefly to remove excess pigmentation.
  4. Acidification: Neutralize with acetic acid.
  5. Staining: Transfer to acid fuchsin stain for approximately 5 minutes.
  6. Dehydration: Pass through an ethanol series (50%, 70%, 95%, 100%).
  7. Clearing: Transfer to clove oil or xylene until transparent.
  8. Mounting: Place a small amount of Canada balsam on a slide. Position the specimen ventral side up (to view diagnostic features). Add cover slip and label.
  9. Drying: Cure slides in a drying oven for at least two weeks.
🌿 Signs on Affected Crops
White Cottony MassesMealybugs appear as white, fluffy, cotton-like clusters on leaves, stems, leaf axils, and fruit.
Sooty MoldHoneydew excreted by mealybugs promotes black sooty mold growth on leaves and fruit, reducing photosynthesis and marketability.
Chlorosis and YellowingFeeding causes chlorosis, leaf yellowing, and reduced vigor.
Defoliation and WiltingPremature leaf fall, wilting, and stunted growth in heavy infestations.
DistortionLeaf and flower bunching; deformation of growing tips.
Root InfestationsSome species also infest roots, causing underground damage.
🌱 Hosts in Dataset
Pawpaw (Malaba) Medinilla (Netherlands)

7. Generic Pest Identification / Entomology Entries

Some entries in the dataset are generic ("Pest Identification", "Entomology", "INSECT", "Scales"). These indicate samples submitted for general entomological identification rather than targeting a specific known pest. For such samples, the diagnostic approach would be:

  1. Visual examination under a stereomicroscope to determine the insect order and family.
  2. Slide mounting of diagnostic structures (genitalia, wings, mouthparts, legs) following the protocols above depending on the insect group.
  3. Molecular identification (COI barcoding) if morphological identification is inconclusive.
  4. Reference to taxonomic keys for the relevant family or genus.
For "Scales" on Pawpaw (Malaba), the protocols for armored scales (Diaspididae) and soft scales (Coccidae) apply. For "INSECT" on Medinilla (Netherlands), the most likely pests based on host records are mealybugs (Pseudococcus, Planococcus) and scale insects, as these are common on Medinilla in greenhouse production.
