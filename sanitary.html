International Standards for Phytosanitary Measures (ISPMs)

▼
📜 What Are ISPMs?

ISPMs are standards adopted by the Commission on Phytosanitary Measures (CPM), the governing body of the International Plant Protection Convention (IPPC). They provide harmonized guidance for protecting plant health while facilitating safe international trade. As of March 2026, there are 47 adopted ISPMs (ISPM 30 revoked), along with 36 Diagnostic Protocols and 46 Phytosanitary Treatments.

Pest Risk Analysis (PRA)

▼
🔬 Definition & Purpose

Pest Risk Analysis (PRA) is the process of evaluating scientific and economic evidence to determine whether an organism is a pest, whether it should be regulated, and what phytosanitary measures, if any, should be taken against it. It is governed by ISPM 2 (Framework for PRA), ISPM 11 (PRA for quarantine pests), and ISPM 21 (PRA for regulated non-quarantine pests).

🧭 Three Stages of PRA (ISPM 2 / ISPM 11)
1Stage 1 — Initiation Identifies the pest or pathway (a specific commodity, a new trade route, or a newly detected organism) that warrants analysis. Triggers include:
  • A new pest discovery or outbreak
  • A request to import a new commodity
  • A review of existing phytosanitary policy
  • A change in pest status in an area
2Stage 2 — Pest Risk Assessment The scientific core of the PRA:
  • Pest categorization — does the organism meet the criteria for a quarantine pest?
  • Probability of introduction — entry, establishment, and spread
  • Probability of spread — natural and human-assisted
  • Magnitude of economic consequences — direct and indirect losses, including environmental and social impacts
3Stage 3 — Pest Risk Management If risk is unacceptable:
  • Identify possible phytosanitary measures
  • Evaluate their effectiveness and feasibility
  • Select measures that achieve the appropriate level of protection (ALOP)
  • Ensure measures are the least restrictive necessary (proportionality)
📊 Example: PRA for a New Fruit Fly Species
PRA ElementExample Assessment
PestBactrocera dorsalis (Oriental fruit fly)
PathwayFresh mango imports from an infested country
Entry potentialHigh — larvae present in fruit; survives transport
Establishment potentialHigh — suitable climate and host plants present
Spread potentialHigh — adults are strong fliers and hitchhike on vehicles
Economic impactSevere — up to 100% loss of susceptible fruit; export market loss
Management optionsSystems approach: field control + post-harvest cold treatment + inspection
Key Principle: PRA must be based on scientific evidence, be transparent, and be documented to allow review by trading partners. Where scientific evidence is insufficient, a country may adopt provisional measures based on the precautionary principle (Article 5.7 of the SPS Agreement), but must seek additional information within a reasonable period.

Pest Free Area (PFA)

▼
🗺️ Definition (ISPM 5)

A Pest Free Area (PFA) is "an area in which a specific pest does not occur as demonstrated by scientific evidence and in which, where appropriate, this condition is being officially maintained." The PFA may be an entire country, part of a country, or an area within a generally infested region. Requirements are set out in ISPM 4.

✅ Requirements for Establishing a PFA (ISPM 4)
1Systems to Establish Freedom Systematic surveys and surveillance following ISPM 6 must demonstrate the pest's absence. For fruit flies, this typically requires trapping for at least 12 consecutive months. For other pests, appropriate sampling and diagnostic protocols (ISPM 27) are used.
2Phytosanitary Measures to Maintain Freedom Once established, measures must prevent pest reintroduction:
  • Controls on movement of regulated articles
  • Public awareness and training
  • Ongoing surveillance and monitoring
  • Border controls and inspection
3Documentation & Review All procedures and evidence must be documented. The PFA status must be regularly reviewed and updated as conditions change. This documentation is essential for trading partners to recognize the PFA (ISPM 29).
📋 Types of Area Freedom
StatusISPMDescription
Pest Free Area (PFA)ISPM 4An area where a specific pest does not occur and is officially maintained as such.
Pest Free Place of Production (PFPP)ISPM 10A defined place of production (e.g., a farm or greenhouse) where a pest is absent and maintained as such.
Pest Free Production Site (PFPS)ISPM 10A subset of a place of production where the pest is absent, with greater physical separation.
Area of Low Pest Prevalence (ALPP)ISPM 22An area where a pest occurs at low levels and is subject to effective surveillance and control.
Pest Free Area for Fruit FliesISPM 26Specific requirements for establishing PFAs for tephritid fruit flies.
Recognition by trading partners (ISPM 29): When a country claims a PFA, the importing country must verify the evidence and may request additional surveys or inspections. The NPPO of the exporting country is responsible for maintaining the PFA and issuing phytosanitary certificates accordingly.

GATT & WTO — The Legal Framework for Phytosanitary Measures

▼
⚖️ The GATT (1947)

The General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT), established in 1947, was the first multilateral agreement to set rules for international trade. It included a general exception in Article XX(b) allowing countries to adopt measures "necessary to protect human, animal or plant life or health." However, this exception was vulnerable to misuse — the GATT dispute settlement panels often found that such measures were applied in a discriminatory or protectionist manner.

🌐 The WTO (1995) and the SPS Agreement

The World Trade Organization (WTO), created in 1995, replaced the GATT Secretariat and introduced the Agreement on the Application of Sanitary and Phytosanitary Measures (SPS Agreement). This agreement clarified and strengthened the rules governing plant health measures in trade.

📋 Key Principles of the SPS Agreement
1. Harmonization WTO members are encouraged to base their national SPS measures on international standards (ISPMs, Codex Alimentarius, OIE/WOAH). Measures conforming to these standards are presumed consistent with the SPS Agreement.
2. Scientific Justification A country may implement a higher standard than an international norm, but must provide scientific justification through a risk assessment (PRA). If scientific evidence is insufficient, provisional measures may be adopted under Article 5.7.
3. Equivalence Members must accept the SPS measures of other countries as equivalent, even if they differ, provided the exporting country demonstrates that its measures achieve the importing country's appropriate level of protection (ALOP).
4. Non-Discrimination SPS measures cannot constitute arbitrary or unjustifiable discrimination between countries where the same conditions prevail. They must be applied consistently to domestic and imported products.
5. Transparency Members must notify new or changed SPS measures, provide copies, and establish an Enquiry Point to answer questions from trading partners.
6. Least Trade-Restrictive Measures Where multiple measures can achieve the ALOP, members should choose the one that is least restrictive to trade (proportionality).
🏛️ Comparison: GATT vs. WTO SPS Agreement
AspectGATT (1947)WTO SPS Agreement (1995)

📅 Historical Timeline
1947 GATT signed — Article XX(b) allows health-protection exceptions, but no detailed rules.
1951 IPPC established under FAO — begins developing phytosanitary standards.
1994 Uruguay Round concludes — WTO created; SPS Agreement adopted.
1995 WTO and SPS Agreement enter into force (1 January 1995).
2000s ISPM series expands — PRA, PFA, systems approaches, and treatment standards developed.
2010s–2020s ePhyto, commodity-specific standards (ISPM 46), and climate-driven pest expansion drive new standards.
Core Outcome: The SPS Agreement recognizes the right of countries to protect plant health, but disciplines that right to ensure it is not used as a disguised trade barrier. Measures must be scientifically justified, transparent, non-discriminatory, and the least restrictive necessary to achieve the desired level of protection.

🌍 Key Institutions
InstitutionRole
IPPC (International Plant Protection Convention)Sets ISPMs; governed by the CPM; housed at FAO in Rome.
CPM (Commission on Phytosanitary Measures)Governing body of the IPPC; adopts ISPMs annually.
NPPO (National Plant Protection Organization)Implements ISPMs at the national level; issues certificates; conducts surveillance and inspection.
WTO (World Trade Organization)Administers the SPS Agreement; provides dispute settlement.

Remember: The IPPC creates the technical standards (ISPMs), while the WTO SPS Agreement provides the legal framework that makes these standards enforceable in international trade. A country's phytosanitary measures must be consistent with both to avoid trade disputes.
