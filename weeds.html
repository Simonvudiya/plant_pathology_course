1. Introduction

1.1 Definition of a Weed

Weeds are plants that grow where they are not wanted (Jethro Tull, 1731); a plant growing where it is not desired (Buchholtz, 1967); any plant that is objectionable or interferes with the activities or welfare of man (Humburg, 1989); any plant or vegetation, excluding fungi, interfering with the objectives or requirements of people (EWRS, 1986).

Other definitions:

  • Herbaceous plant not valued for use or beauty, growing wild and rank, regarded as cumbering the ground or hindering growth of superior vegetation (Little et al., 1973).
  • A plant that originated in a natural environment and, in response to imposed or natural environments, evolved, and continues to do so, as an interfering associate with our crops and activities (Aldrich, 1984).
  • Navas (1991): a plant that forms populations able to enter habitats cultivated, markedly disturbed or occupied by man, and potentially depress or displace resident plant populations deliberately cultivated or of ecological/aesthetic interest.
Most definitions emphasise behaviour that affects humans. Aldrich wants us to recognise weeds as part of a dynamic, not static, ecosystem — departing from definitions that regard weeds as enemies to be controlled.

1.2 Origin of Weeds

Two of the earliest references on the effects of weed competition appear in ancient writings of the Bible:

"Cursed is the ground for thy sake; in sorrow shalt thou eat of it all the days of thy life; thorns also and thistles shall it bring forth to thee…" (Genesis 3:17–18)
"Some fell among thorns; and the thorns sprang up, and choked them" (Matthew 13:7)
  • Without man there would be no weeds.
  • Weeds thrive best in disturbed environments.
  • Ploughing encourages several weed species.
  • Man is the greatest disturber of the environment ever.
  • Weed control is as old as agriculture.
  • Weed control is the most expensive step in crop production.
  • The good tropical environment (high rainfall, temperature, fertile soil) encourages prolific weed growth.
  • Man has done very little to fight weed problems.

1.3 Systematic Progress in Weed Control

  1. Fingers (hand pulling)
  2. Sharpened sticks to replace fingers
  3. Sharpened stones (flints)
  4. Metal hoes or machetes
  5. Metal hoes tied to a horse or oxen
  6. Planting of crops in rows to facilitate horses/oxen hoeing — development of ploughs, cultivators and rotary weeders. Row planting started about 200–250 years ago.
  7. Chemicals. Chemical energy is soon replacing mechanical energy for weed control due to cost and unavailability of labour.

1.4 Weeds Include the Following Vegetation

  • Trees (shrubs and herbs)
  • Broadleaf weed species
  • Grasses
  • Sedges
  • Aquatic plants (any of the above) e.g. water hyacinth (Eichhornia crassipes)
  • Parasitic plants e.g. witch weed (Striga spp.), dodder (Cuscuta spp.)
  • Many modern weeds did not exist before agriculture — these are not indigenous and are introductions.
  • Crop plants may have arisen from weeds by mutation to gigantism or through breeding.
  • Compare cultivated carrots (bigger than wild carrots) and watermelon (bigger than wild cucumber).
  • Man gave care to cultivated ones, hence they grew bigger.

Weeds therefore may come from:

  1. Wild species long adapted to the site of disturbance.
  2. New species or varieties or ecotypes that have evolved since agriculture.
Weeds that resemble crops (mimicry) are more difficult to control than those that do not.

1.5 Weed–Crop Mimicry

Definition: Phenomenon where weeds develop such close resemblance to some phases of the life history of a crop as to be mistaken for the crop, and thus evade eradication (Barrett, 1983).

Types of Mimicry

  1. Vegetative mimicry: Similarity in appearance between weeds and crops at seedling and vegetative stages.
    • Seedlings of perennial wild rice (Oryza longistamina) and cultivated rice
    • Wild cane (Saccharum spontaneum) in cultivated sugarcane
    • Wild sorghum (Sorghum halepense) seedlings in cultivated sorghum
    • Rice mimicry in barnyard grass (Echinochloa crus-galli)
  2. Seed mimicry: Resemblance between crop seed and weed seed in size, colour, shape and weight — cannot be separated by sieving, hand picking, specific gravity (floatation), etc.
  3. Biochemical mimicry: The weed develops resistance to herbicides that were hitherto used to control it selectively in a given crop.

2. Ecology, Characteristics & Importance of Weeds

2.1 Environmental Factors

The environment is characterised by: Climate, Edaphic (soil) and Biotic factors.

(1) Climate

  • Light: Necessary for germination (some seeds require light, some light-neutral, others require darkness/burial); required for photosynthesis and growth; needed for flowering (photoperiodism) — governs flower and seed production.
  • Rainfall: Provides water as precipitation, fog or dew. Water is significant for germination (imbibition is the first stage — seed swells, testa breaks, enzymes break complex sugars to simple sugars for growth). Needed for photosynthesis, growth and reproduction. Disseminates weeds from place to place. Amount of water determines types of weeds (aquatic vs terrestrial).
  • Temperature: Affects germination, growth, reproduction and survival of underground plant parts. Also affects dissemination by forceful dehiscence (shattering) in pod-bearing weeds.
  • Wind: Balances distribution of oxygen, nitrogen and carbon dioxide. Responsible for dispersal of weed seeds (with parachute-like structures). Increases evapo-transpiration, particularly at high temperature.

(2) Edaphic Factors

  • Soil pH: Acid, neutral or alkaline soils. Some weeds grow in alkaline soils e.g. Cynodon dactylon. Each weed has an optimal pH where germination, growth and reproduction is maximum.
  • Soil Fertility: Presence of essential plant nutrients. Appearance of weeds can indicate soil fertility — dark green, large, healthy leaves on robust weeds indicate high fertility (nitrogen). Pigweed (Amaranthus spp.) grows in high-fertility soils. Fertile soils support germination, fast growth and higher seed production in weeds.
  • Soil Temperature: Fluctuation is less than in ambient temperature. Optimal temperature required for each weed species. Extreme temperatures reduce germination, growth, reproduction and survival.
  • Soil Water: Necessary for germination, growth and reproduction. Some weeds grow in waterlogged soils (aquatic), others under rainfed conditions (terrestrial). Weeds with cell sap grow in soils with low water content e.g. Euphorbia, Aloe spp.
  • Aeration: Oxygen required for seed and root respiration. Required for germination and growth. Absence under water-logging affects terrestrial weeds. Soil compaction reduces air availability.

(3) Biotic Factors

  • Plants can compete with weeds for essential growth factors, reducing weed growth and reproduction.
  • Plants can exude allelochemicals that interfere with growth, development and reproduction of weeds.
  • Plants can harbour insect pests, diseases and nematodes that affect weeds.
  • Animals/rodents can graze and trample on weeds, reducing their growth.
  • Soil microorganisms can influence nutrient availability and disease prevalence.
  • Man through his activities influences weed growth, reproduction and dissemination.

2.2 Characteristics of Weeds

No one weed possesses all these characteristics, but together they explain weed success:

  1. High output of seeds in favourable conditions.
  2. At least some output of seeds even in very poor conditions.
  3. Seed production begins after only a short period of vegetative growth.
  4. Seed production spreads over a long period of the growth of the plant.
  5. Variable seed dormancy and considerable longevity of seeds in soil.
  6. No special environmental requirement for germination.
  7. Rapid seedling growth and establishment.
  8. Wide tolerance of variations in the physical environment (soil etc.), even winter.
  9. Adaptations for both long and short distance dispersal.
  10. Good powers of vegetative reproduction and ability to regenerate when divided into fragments.
  11. Self-compatibility (any plant able to set seeds in the absence of other plants of the same species). Cross-pollination is advantageous if pollinator is wind or insects with non-specialised habits.
  12. Strong competitive ability.

2.3 Economic Importance of Weeds

Positive Values

  1. Supply organic matter ploughed under soil.
  2. Medicinal purposes e.g. Ageratum conyzoides (stops bleeding), Albizia anthelmintica (antiworms), Aloe spp. (skin, stomach and others), Azadirachta indica (malaria etc., "Mua Arobaini" claimed to treat 40 diseases).
  3. Check erosion (binding agents in un-ploughed hilly areas).
  4. Genetic material for breeding.
  5. Forage for livestock and bedding.
  6. Beauty (source of weed introduction).
  7. Aromatic preparations e.g. Cyperus spp. (tubers used for "agar-batties" — sweet-scented sticks for burning).
  8. Host for beneficial insects.
  9. Some weeds are anti-bloat.
  10. Nectar for bees.
  11. Recycling nutrients from deep in the soil.
  12. Allelochemics e.g. Tagetes minuta suppresses nematodes.
  13. Household use — baskets, hats, garbage containers, trays e.g. Sida acuta.
  14. Wild weeds used as vegetables e.g. Asystasia & chimperi (Asystasia), Solanum nigrum (black nightshade), Gynandropsis gynandra / Cleome gynandra (spider plant), Amaranthus hybridus (pigweed), Commelina benghalensis (wandering jew).

Negative Values of Weeds

  1. Reduction of crop yields due to competition for light, moisture, space, carbon dioxide, nutrients and allelopathic effects.
  2. Interferes with agricultural operations (damage and functioning of farm machinery e.g. knot grass with long wiry stem).
  3. Increase in labour cost — crops infested with weeds require extra labour; weeds cause breakages, stoppages and extra depreciation of harvesting machinery.
  4. Poor quality agricultural produce — weeds with grains impair quality and lower prices. Grading in wheat is done according to percentage of foreign matter (weed seed and inert materials). Fodder crops harvested with certain weeds e.g. wild garlic impart objectionable odour to milk (even Tagetes minuta).
  5. Entanglement of weed seeds e.g. black jack (Bidens pilosa) reduces value of cotton fibre.
  6. Less efficient use of land — weeds reduce carrying capacity of grazing lands and cause depreciation of land value (especially perennial weeds).
  7. Difficulty in water management — create problems in irrigation and drainage systems (choking) e.g. water hyacinth (Eichhornia crassipes) and Salvinia molesta in Lake Victoria and Lake Naivasha respectively. Aquatic weeds deplete nutrients in water, obstruct fish, hinder fishing operations, swimming and navigation.
  8. Flowering weeds compete with crops in attracting bees, thus interfering with pollination and yield of crops.
  9. Cause health hazards to human and livestock:
    • Castor bean (Ricinus communis) seed contains deadly poison RICIN — as little as 10 micrograms is enough to kill a 90 kg man. Must enter bloodstream through abrasions, cuts or mucous membranes of intestinal tract (more deadly than snake venom).
    • Some weeds accumulate high nitrate (NO₃) levels poisonous to livestock e.g. lambsquarters (Chenopodium album), nightshade (Solanum nigrum), pigweed (Amaranthus spp.). Ruminants are more susceptible to NO₃ poisoning than monogastrics (pigs and man).
    • Some weeds are parasitic and draw nutrients from the host plant e.g. Striga spp., dodder, Orobanche and mistletoe.
  10. Weeds act as alternate hosts to diseases, insect pests and nematodes.

3. Plant Interference & Allelopathy

3.1 Forms of Plant Interference

  1. Competition (allelospoly): Depletion of one or more limiting resources such as light, a nutrient or water.
  2. Allelomediation: Effects on the physical or biological environment that interfere with growth of a neighbouring plant e.g. harbouring herbivores that selectively graze certain species in the association.
  3. Allelopathy: Production of chemicals by living or decaying plant tissue which interferes with growth of a susceptible neighbouring plant.

3.2 Allelopathy

Definition: Allelopathy is "biochemical interaction between plants" (Annual Review of Phytopathology 16:431). One plant (donor) secretes growth inhibitory or stimulatory substances (allelochemicals) into its environment which interfere with growth and development of a sensitive neighbour (recipient) when taken up.

Mode of Action of Allelochemicals

  1. Inhibit crop seed germination.
  2. Formation of crop seedlings which are deformed or abnormal (epinasty).
  3. Inhibition of cell division and elongation (Nishida et al., 2005 — monoterpenoids affected cell proliferation and DNA synthesis in plant meristems).
  4. Cellular disorganisation in roots; distorts shape and structure of plant cells (Palowski et al., 2012).
  5. Imbalance in the antioxidant systems (Ding et al., 2007).
  6. Increase in cell membrane permeability (Harun et al., 2014).
  7. Affects plant growth regulation systems — alter contents of plant growth regulators or induce imbalances in phytohormones (Yang et al., 2005).
  8. Affects photosynthesis — inhibit or damage synthesis machinery and accelerate decomposition of photosynthetic pigments (Wu et al., 2015).
  9. Influences respiration — electron transfer in mitochondria, oxidative phosphorylation, CO₂ generation, ATP enzyme activity (Fang and Zhihui, 2016).

Examples

  • Scarcity of other broadleaf plants near leafy spurge (Euphorbia esula) colonies.
  • Datura stramonium inhibited elongation of cells in cabbage roots when grown together in agar in a test tube; also induced cellular disorganisation of root tissues.
  • Green foxtail (Setaria viridis) seedlings disrupted normal growth of cabbage root.
  • Leachates of foxtail seedlings and mature/dead foxtail plants reduced dry weight of maize.
  • Black walnut (Juglans nigra) contains a phytotoxic chemical that kills or injures plants such as alfalfa (lucerne), apple and tomato growing close to its roots.

3.3 Proof of Allelopathy

No study has followed a set of criteria similar to Koch's postulates to provide undisputable proof (Fuerst and Putnam, 1983). Necessary steps:

  1. Demonstrate the existence of interference, describe symptoms and quantify degree of interference.
  2. Isolate, assay, characterise and synthesise the toxins.
  3. Repeat the symptoms by application of the toxins at rates present in nature at appropriate stages of susceptible plant growth.
  4. Monitor and show release, movement and uptake of the toxin is sufficient for observed interference.

Also essential: consider selectivity of the toxin by comparing range of species affected by the isolated toxin to the range showing allelopathic injury when grown with the agent (Stowe, 1979). In nature several compounds may act jointly; synergistic effects implicated for organic acids and phenolic compounds (Evanari, 1949).

Altieri and Doll (1978) suggested the following work before any attempt to control weeds by allelopathy:

  1. Identification of allelopathic interactions between: (a) crops and crops, (b) crops and weeds, (c) weeds and weeds, (d) seed to seed, (e) plant to seed, (f) plant to plant, (g) residue to seed, (h) residue to plant.
  2. Identification of the parts of plants which contain inhibitors and ways they are released into the environment.
  3. Effects of residues from plants of different ages and from different plant parts.
  4. Identification of possible synergisms and/or antagonism between incorporated residues.
  5. Evaluation of effects of environmental factors and cultural practices on behaviour of inhibitors.
  6. Chemical identification of the inhibitors and their mechanisms of action.

3.4 Practical Application of Allelopathy in the Field

  1. Application of mulches of fresh residues e.g. Tagetes patula in recently planted maize field.
  2. Spraying extract from plant parts combining high concentrations of inhibitors. Determine optimum time of application and duration of effect.
  3. Planting strips of species which actively release substances to weeds but not to the crops under study.
  4. Integration of allelopathic strategies with other methods of weed control.
  5. Evaluation of the effect of hormonal stimulation on inhibitor production in crops or specific weeds.
  6. Genetic incorporation of allelopathic properties into commercial cultivars of the main crops (Putnam and Duke, 1974).

3.5 Sources and Release of Allelochemicals

Allelochemicals are virtually present in all plant tissues including leaves, stems, roots, rhizomes, flowers, fruits and seeds. Critical question: are the compounds released in sufficient quantities to elicit a response?

Release Mechanisms

  1. Volatilisation: Plants in arid regions release volatiles e.g. Artemisia, Eucalyptus, Salvia spp. Volatile compounds identified include terpenoids (monoterpenes and sesquiterpenes).
  2. Root exudation: Several compounds released by plant roots. Common lambsquarters (Chenopodium album) exudes toxic levels of oxalic acid at flowering stages.
  3. Leaching: May occur from aerial portions by rainwater, fog or drip. Compounds leached include organic acids, sugars, amino acids, pectic substances, gibberellic acids, terpenoids, alkaloids and phenolic compounds.
  4. Decomposition of plant residues: Chemicals released directly by leaching of litter after death. Microbes in the rhizosphere may induce production of toxic compounds by enzymatic degradation. Example: action by microbes on cyanogenic glycosides of johnson grass (Sorghum halepense) and Prunus species produce HCN and benzaldehydes.

4. Classification of Weeds

4.1 On the Basis of Life Cycle

(a) Annuals

Complete their life cycle in one growing season (one year or less). Take one year or less from germination to seed production, then die. Easy to control by tillage or chemicals. Roots have no power of regeneration.

  • Amaranthus hybridus (pigweed) — annual broadleaf
  • Tagetes minuta (Mexican marigold) — annual broadleaf
  • Bidens pilosa (black jack) — annual broadleaf
  • Avena fatua (wild oat) — annual grass

Because of prolific seed production, annuals are abundant, very persistent and have high cost of control. Many field weeds are annuals.

(b) Biennials

Require two growing seasons (bimodal rainfall) or 2 years (unimodal rainfall) to complete their life cycle. First year they form rosettes (radial cluster of leaves close to the ground). Second year they send up flower stalks (bolting), set viable seeds and die. Not as common as annual weeds.

  • Daucus carota — wild carrot
  • Melilotus spp. — sweet clover
  • Pastinaca sativa — wild parsnip

(c) Perennials

Live for 3 or more years. Propagate and spread by seeds (sexual) or vegetatively (asexual). Asexual propagation makes them difficult to control because of hidden underground parts.

Herbaceous Perennials

(i) Simple Perennials — Usually produce by seeds except when cut.

  • Sonchus arvensis — perennial sow thistle
  • Taraxacum officinale — dandelion

(ii) Creeping Perennials — Reproduce primarily by vegetative means such as rhizomes (below-ground stem), stolons (above-ground stem), spreading roots. May also reproduce by seed.

  • Convolvulus arvensis — field bindweed
  • Sorghum halepense — johnson grass
  • Pennisetum clandestinum — Kikuyu grass
  • Cynodon dactylon — star grass

Creeping perennials are the most difficult to control due to multiple reproduction mechanisms. Have powers to regenerate when fragmented.

(iii) Tuber Perennials — Propagation primarily by tubers; can also reproduce by seeds.

  • Cyperus rotundus — purple nut sedge (propagates by tubers only)
  • Cyperus esculentus — yellow nut sedge (propagates by both tubers and seeds)

Note: Tubers are enlarged fleshy rhizomes (storage structures).

(iv) Bulbous Perennials — Propagation primarily by bulbs and bulbils; can also reproduce by seeds.

  • Allium spp. — wild onion and garlic
  • Oxalis spp.

Note: Bulbs are large, fleshy leafy bases used for storage.

(v) Woody Perennials — Consist of trees, bushes and shrubs. May not produce seeds for a few years but once mature produce seeds every year.

4.2 On the Basis of Growth Characteristics

(i) Erect

Stems upright; leaves exposed; not supported.

  • Chenopodium album — fat hen
  • Tagetes minuta — Mexican marigold

(ii) Prostrate

Short stems with extremely short internodes (look like crown of leaves borne on roots).

  • Eleusine indica — wild finger millet

(iii) Twining

Coils around support.

  • Cuscuta spp. — dodder
  • Convolvulus arvensis — field bindweed

(iv) Trailing

Stems spread on the ground.

  • Commelina benghalensis — wandering jew

(v) Runners

Stems grow horizontally along the ground surface; form special roots; root at the nodes.

  • Commelina benghalensis — wandering jew

4.3 On the Basis of Habitat

(i) Weeds of Cultivated (Arable) Lands

Life cycle coincides with that of the crop.

  • Amaranthus spp. — pigweed

(ii) Weeds of Lawns and Public Parks

Found in standard lawns, golf clubs; mainly spreading grasses.

  • Cynodon dactylon — star grass

(iii) Orchard Weeds

Grow under fruit trees. Microclimate characterised by shade, high humidity, excessive moisture and low temperature.

  • Cannabis sativa — bang
  • Bidens pilosa — black jack
  • Solanum nigrum — black nightshade

(iv) Aquatic Weeds

Grow in water bodies or water-saturated areas (marshy areas).

  • Pistia stratiotes — water lettuce
  • Salvinia molesta — salvinia
  • Eichhornia crassipes — water hyacinth

(v) Pasture Weeds

Frequently found in cultivated areas.

  • Tagetes minuta — Mexican marigold
  • Galinsoga parviflora — gallant soldier
  • Solanum incanum — Sodom apple
  • Avena fatua — wild oat

(vi) Roadside Weeds

Found in waste areas; include annuals, biennials and perennials; hardy and survive harsh environments.

  • Euphorbia spp.

(vii) Weeds of Uncultivated Lands

Hardy weeds.

  • Digitaria scalarum — couch grass

4.4 On the Basis of Morphology

(a) Monocotyledons

Comprise mainly members of Gramineae (Poaceae) family; mostly grasses. Have hollow stems or readily removed pith, sometimes solid. Nodes closed and usually hard. Blades usually linear or lanceolate. Narrow, parallel-veined leaves. Ligule usually present. Growing points hidden in perennial grasses (difficult to control).

  • Eleusine indica — wild finger millet
  • Cynodon dactylon — star grass
  • Avena fatua — wild oat
  • Pennisetum clandestinum — Kikuyu grass

(b) Dicotyledons

Comprise broadleaved weed species. Growing points exposed (easy to control). Have simple or compound leaves. Broad leaves with branched veins. This classification is important in chemical control.

  • Amaranthus spp. — pigweed
  • Solanum nigrum — black nightshade
  • Chenopodium album — fat hen
  • Bidens pilosa — black jack

(c) Sedges

Belong to family Cyperaceae. Similar to grasses in some characteristics. Have three-ranked flowers. Have fibrous roots. Solid stem.

  • Cyperus rotundus
  • Cyperus esculentus

5. Weed Seed Germination & Dormancy

5.1 Germination

Definition: Seed germination is the emergence and development of essential structures capable of producing a normal plant under favourable conditions (Howell, 1960).

Internal Conditions to the Seed

  1. Adequate food supply.
  2. Metabolic systems capable of utilising food supply.
  3. Proper balance of growth hormones.
  4. Facility to transport chemicals vital to life and growth (hormones, nutrients).

External Conditions to the Seed

  1. Water
  2. Temperature
  3. Oxygen (aeration)
  4. Light — its presence or absence
  5. In some cases parasitic weeds e.g. Striga spp., dodder (Cuscuta spp.) require a germination stimulant supplied by the host plant before germination

(1) Moisture

  • First stage in germination is imbibition.
  • Seed swells, increasing size by 25–200% owing to water uptake.
  • Expanding seed breaks seed coat.
  • High rate of enzyme activity.
  • Breakdown of insoluble food reserves to soluble ones.
  • In dry soil the seed remains dormant.

(2) Oxygen

  • Essential for respiration (energy-producing).
  • Water-logging renders oxygen inadequate, hence no respiration followed by rotting due to attack by anaerobic microbes.

(3) Temperature

  • Requirement for seed germination coincides with those associated with active plant growth.
  • Minimum temperature: Below which germination will not occur. Enzymes are inactive; below 0°C.
  • Maximum temperature: Above which germination will not occur. Enzymes denatured; cell protein coagulation; above 48°C.
  • Optimum temperature: Ideal temperature when seeds germinate fastest; coincides with maximum enzymatic activity.
  • Based on temperature you have cool and warm season weed spp.
  • High temperature can force weeds into "secondary dormancy" — may be an oxygen relationship but not clear.

(4) Light

  • Presence or absence of light may either favour or inhibit germination.
  • Some seeds germinate best in light e.g. tobacco, primrose (require burial).
  • Some germinate best in darkness e.g. onion, lily (left on soil surface).
  • Others germinate readily in either light or darkness e.g. clover, wheat (most crops).
  • Location of seeds near or on the soil surface may have a direct effect on ultimate germination.
  • Length of day (duration) and quality (colour) of light have influence.

5.2 Seed Dormancy

A type of resting stage for the seed. The seeds fail to germinate owing to factors associated with their seed or environmental conditions. It is difficult to determine under natural conditions whether the seed is dormant or merely quiescent (inactive).

Types of Seed Dormancy

  • (a) Innate dormancy: Due to genetically impermeable seed coat to oxygen, water or both; due to resistant hard seed coat (restricts emergence of radicle and plumule); seeds may have chemical endogenous inhibitors; seeds may also have rudimentary embryo.
  • (b) Induced dormancy: Depends on interaction between seed and the environment. Brought about by unfavourable environmental conditions e.g. lack of moisture, high CO₂ pressure, low O₂ pressure, lack/presence of light, inappropriate temperature limit, lack of germination stimulants for parasitic weeds.

Factors Affecting Seed Dormancy

  1. Seed coat impermeable to water or oxygen or both e.g. velvet leaf (Abutilon theophrasti) and annual morning glory (Ipomoea spp.). If seed coat impermeable, H₂O and/or O₂ do not reach the first step in germination. Hard seed coat e.g. pigweed (Amaranthus spp.) and mustard (Brassica spp.). Readily permeable to water within limits. Looks shiny if seeds are rubbed between index finger and thumb. Seed coat is too strong and developing embryo remains confined.
  2. Immature embryo: Some plant species drop or shed their seeds while the embryo is still immature. The seed is dormant until the embryo completes its morphological development.
  3. Embryos that require an "after-ripening period" e.g. in grasses and roses. Embryos are fully formed but require more time to complete physiological development — a process called after-ripening. Seeds remain dormant till after-ripening is completed.
  4. Endogenous chemical germination inhibitors: Found in all parts of the seed including embryo, endosperm, nucellus, seed coat (testa) and pericarp. In general the chemical inhibitors are water soluble.
  5. Inappropriate environmental conditions.
  6. Lack of germination stimulants.

Treatment for Impermeable or Hard Seed Coats

  • Physical scarification: Artificial — any activity that causes abrasion. Rub with sandpaper if seeds are large and few; scratch with a knife; prick with needle; mix seed with sand in ratio 1:2 in a plastic container and agitate. Once hard seed coat absorbs H₂O the seed swells and cracks.
  • Chemical scarification: Soak in concentrated sulfuric or hydrochloric acid for 5 seconds. Prolonged soaking kills the seed.

Treatment for Immature and After-Ripening Embryo

  • Store in a dry, cool place for at least 6 months.

Treatment for Endogenous Germination Inhibitors

  • Soak in water or alcohol (inhibitors are soluble in water or alcohol).

Treatment for Inappropriate Environmental Conditions

  • Provide appropriate artificial conditions e.g. oxygen, light or darkness.

Treatment for Lack of Germination Stimulant

  • Necessary for parasitic weeds. Provide artificial germination stimulants e.g. ethylene, strigol analogues (GR 24).

Natural Mechanisms of Breaking Dormancy

  • Ploughing causes abrasion.
  • Gnawing by rodents, insects, cockroaches, ants in/on the soil.
  • Soil erosion that hits weed seeds on hard surfaces.
  • Attack by microorganisms.
  • Rainfall and leaching in soils dissolves endogenous germination inhibitors.
  • Change in temperatures from cold to hot.

Advantages of Dormancy in Plants

  1. Survival mechanism — germinates only when conditions are favourable for seed survival.
  2. Tends to regulate germination of seed populations, ensuring a reservation of un-germinated but viable seed for later seasons.

Although ploughing triggers germination of weed seeds, some remain dormant in the soil. Almost without exception the seeds of weed species possess one or more of the factors contributing to seed dormancy. This brings the problem of weed control. Factors contributing to dormancy have been removed from seeds of crop plants by selective breeding to facilitate rapid (ahead of weeds) and uniform germination (to facilitate mechanical harvesting).

6. Weed–Crop Competition

Interference: Collective interactions that occur among plants growing close together.

6.1 Types of Interactions

  • Competition: The struggle between two organisms for a limited resource essential for growth.
  • Allelopathy: The release of chemicals by one plant that suppresses growth of adjacent plants.
  • Parasitism: A parasitic plant acquires nutrients through direct association with a host plant rather than competing with it. Parasitic weeds are usually much more damaging to crops than other weeds. Examples: dodder, Striga (witch weed), Orobanche and mistletoe.
  • Shade avoidance response: The change in red:far-red light caused when sunlight is reflected off foliage of a plant is detected by phytochrome of adjacent plants. Plants growing near other plants may alter growth habit (grow taller) in response to the other plant. The change in resource allocation due to altered growth habit may influence the ability of the crop to respond to other factors later in the season. For example, if the presence of weeds early in the growing season resulted in maize growing taller, the resources required to achieve the greater height likely came at the cost of the root system. This reallocation might be detrimental if the growing season turns out dry later.

6.2 Nature of Weed–Crop Competition

Several factors may be limiting to plant growth, including: water, light, nutrients, carbon dioxide and space. In most agronomic situations, water and light are resources which weeds and crops compete for most, as nutrients may be naturally available in the soil. Recent research has shown weeds may compete with maize for nitrogen early in the season.

Growth habit of crop and weed determines which plant has an advantage for a specific resource:

  • Tall plants are typically more competitive for light than low-growing plants, thus light competition is more important in soybean than in maize in most situations.
  • The root system of weeds varies widely among species. Cocklebur (Xanthium spp.) has an extensive root system that acquires water from a larger area than many other weeds, increasing its competitiveness.

6.3 Factors Influencing Crop/Weed Competition

Friesen (1967) and Sagar (1968) posed the following questions:

  • What weed densities are necessary to reduce crop yields?
  • Do similar densities have similar effects in all crops?
  • At what stages of development does competition occur?
  • What is the influence of fertility and moisture?
  • How important is delay in sowing (or emergence) of the crop in determining the outcome of competition?
  • How reproducible are the effects of weeds from field to field, area to area, and country to country?
  • How do different species (or populations) of weeds compare?
  • What is the extent of yield loss associated with farmers' traditional weeding methods, timing and frequency?

Weed–crop competition is divided into two:

  • Above ground: Involves leaves and plant competes for light and carbon dioxide. Carbon dioxide does not limit if there is air circulation which restores atmospheric concentration.
  • Below ground: Involves plant roots; growth factors involved include water, nutrients and oxygen.

6.4 Factors that Affect Competition

1) Weed Factors

(a) Effect of weed species: Competitive ability varies with weed species, density and weight, and duration of infestation.

Why are some weeds more competitive than others?

  • They germinate quickly with high seedling vigour e.g. many annual tropical grass weeds.
  • Have higher seedling growth rate than other weeds and crops e.g. Euphorbia heterophylla.
  • Develop canopy quickly and shade out other weeds and crops e.g. Chromolaena odorata.
  • Have biochemical adaptations for more efficient photosynthetic pathways.

(b) Effect of weed density and weight:

  • Effect of weed density on crop yield is a sigmoid curve (crop yield is reduced to zero before maximum weed density is attained).
  • Effect of weed weight on crop yield is a linear inverse relationship (as weed weight increases, crop yield decreases).
  • There is a period during early crop establishment when growth factors have not become limiting and very low weed densities have no effect on crop yield.

(c) Effect of onset and duration of weed–crop association:

  • There is a stage in the seedling of weed and crop when negative interaction has not set in (when developing roots, root hairs and leaves).
  • Studies show that moisture is implicated in early weed competition (the first 3 weeks after emergence) before other growth factors become limiting.
  • Most farmers do not weed their crops at this stage, hence yield loss due to untimely weeding is real.
  • The first weeding should not be delayed longer than when weeds are at the 6th leaf stage (or more than 3 weeks for most crops).
  • Any delay will be reflected in yield loss.
  • Generally 2 properly spaced hand weedings within 8 weeks for maize, sorghum and millet and within 6 weeks for most legumes results in yields equivalent to weed-free.

(d) Effect of weeds on growth factors:

  • Competitive effect of weeds is a result of their effect on growth factors (water, nutrient, light).
  • Water use efficiency is a measure of the amount of water required by a plant to produce 1 g of dry matter.
  • Examples: Maize needs 349 g; sorghum 304 g (more efficient) compared with beans 569 g and sunflower 623 g (non-efficient).
  • Amaranthus retroflexus, 305 g and Portulaca oleracea, 281 g (more efficient) and Chenopodium album 658 g and Polygonum aviculare, 678 g (non-efficient).
  • In periods of moisture stress, crops that make more efficient use of water will yield more than less efficient crops.
  • Weeds that are more efficient in water use (lower water requirement) will have a competitive advantage over less efficient crops.
  • Root development governs water availability. Davis et al. (1967) showed that competition for water between crops and weeds is affected by relative root volume occupied by each.
  • If weed and crop have extensive lateral root systems, competition for water will be more severe than if one has extensive lateral and the other has deep vertical root distribution.
  • Amount of seasonal water supply also affects water use by plants.
  • Light is an important growth factor competed for by weeds and crops. Ability to grow rapidly and develop canopy cover is important for successful competition for light. In absence of light, the shaded crop is unable to produce its needs of assimilates. This reduction in assimilate quantity reduces root/shoot ratio in the crop. As a result the ability of the crop to absorb water and nutrients is diminished.

2) Crop Factors

  • (a) Type of crop and seeding rate: Many crop plants have inherent competitive ability against weeds; influenced by crop type and seeding rate. If growth factors are not limiting and crops are established in a weed-free environment, most crops would give good yields at optimum population.
  • (b) Spatial arrangement of crops: Row spacing plays an important role. Soybean, maize, cowpea and rice are crops in which changes in row spacing improve competitive ability. Period required for weed control in cotton planted at 53 cm row spacing was 6 weeks and at 106 cm was 14 weeks (Rogers et al., 1976). In groundnut, yield increase and better competitive ability was registered with narrow row spacing (Buchanan et al., 1976).
  • (c) Plant architecture: Important in long-season crops e.g. cassava. Better weed control was achieved in early branching cassava varieties than in late branching ones (Akobundu, 1980). Profusely tillering rice varieties e.g. Andy II compete better with weeds than moderate tillering ones e.g. ITA 123 (Akobundu and Ahissou, 1985).
  • (d) Availability of growth factors: Adverse effects of weeds are more pronounced when growth factors are limiting. A shortage of one factor creates imbalance that interferes with uptake and utilisation of other factors. Crops differ in their requirements for growth factors. Plant height is important in competition for light. Shallow-rooting crops are more susceptible to moisture stress than deep-rooting ones.
  • (e) Cropping patterns: All crops irrespective of growth patterns are susceptible to early weed competition. Multiple cropping, because of better ground cover, suppresses weeds compared to mono-cropping. Research (Akobundu, 1981b) reported more weed biomass in maize monocrop than in maize–cassava intercrop.

3) Environmental Factors

  • (a) Climate: Important climatic factors are water and light. Seasonal distribution, quality (colour) and quantity (duration) affect plant growth.
  • (b) Tillage: Effectiveness of tillage method in controlling weeds is paramount. Tillage favours annual grass and broadleaf weeds. No tillage encourages growth of perennial weeds especially woody species.
  • (c) Soil fertility: Affects severity of weed–crop competition. If weeds are established in a weed-free environment, subsequent weed growth affects more crops grown in low-fertility soils.
  • (d) Ground cover management: Mulching (dead or live) is an effective method of weed control by smothering weeds (cuts off light). Mulch can also release allelochemicals that control other weeds. Type and depth of mulch that is effective should be determined. Crops effective as live mulch should be determined.

6.5 Weed–Crop Competition for Growth Factors

The growth factors that weeds and crops compete for are mainly water, nutrients and light.

(1) Competition for Water

Weeds consume a lot of water; most water is lost by transpiration. An experiment estimated amounts consumed by weeds compared to certain crops:

  • One (1) Russian thistle (Salsola iberica) consumed as much water as three (3) sorghum plants.
  • One (1) mustard (Brassica spp.) as much as four (4) oat plants.
  • One (1) sunflower (Helianthus annus) plant as much as two and a half (2.5) maize plants.

Removal of these weeds means water saving.

(2) Competition for Nutrients

An experiment estimated amount of nutrient consumed by each of 5 weeds compared to maize. Weeds used:

  1. Pigweed (Amaranthus hybridus)
  2. Lambsquarter (Chenopodium album)
  3. Smartweed (Polygonum spp.)
  4. Crab grass (Digitaria spp.)

On analysis, each weed species contained a higher percentage of nutrients than maize. On dry weight basis, the weeds contained:

  • Twice (2×) as much Nitrogen
  • One and a half (1.5×) times more Phosphorus
  • Three and a half (3.5×) times more Potassium
  • Seven and a half (7.5×) times more Calcium
  • Three (3×) times more Magnesium

For nutrients, absolute amount, available amount, period of availability and scarcity are important.

(3) Competition for Light

  • Under plant competition where water and nutrients are adequate, low light intensity such as from shading is a major factor limiting plant growth.
  • Competition for light among plants involves positioning of leaves with respect to light interception.
  • Successful competitors for light start growth earlier, grow taller than neighbours.
  • Broadleaved weeds have advantage over narrow-leaved (grass) plants in competing for light.
  • Over-shadowing of shorter plants by taller ones is the principal means of light competition.
  • Climbing vine weeds e.g. morning glory (Ipomoea spp.) and field bindweed (Convolvulus arvensis) ascend neighbouring plants, forming a tangled mat over these plants and position their leaves for light interception.
  • Field beans (Phaseolus vulgaris) plants are approximately 43 cm while lambsquarters (Chenopodium album) and Mexican marigold (Tagetes minuta) are approximately 2 m when mature, therefore shade the beans.
  • Shading is the primary cause of yield reduction in beans due to weed competition.
  • Often there are dramatic differences in competitive ability between plants of the same species (varietal differences exist).
  • Shade-tolerant weed species have an advantage e.g. Solanum nigrum, Bidens pilosa and Cannabis sativa.
  • Weeds of similar growth habits as the crop are often more serious competitors than weeds of a dissimilar growth habit.

7. Dissemination of Weeds

Distribution of weeds throughout the world has been associated directly with man's exploration and colonisation of distant places. Man moved with familiar food plants and seed to sustain him and his livestock. Inadvertently through ignorance or oversight he carried with him seeds of weeds common to the region from which he travelled. The seeds were "stowaways" among his goods and foodstuffs. Same as human diseases e.g. diphtheria, measles, smallpox, tuberculosis, HIV/AIDS.

Weed seeds were in:

  • Hay for livestock
  • Vegetative matter for packaging
  • Between boards of boxes and crates
  • Fastened on clothing
  • On hair or wool of domestic animals

Weeds spread from place to place by dispersal of their reproductive parts such as seeds (sexual) and vegetative propagules (bulbs, corms, roots, rhizomes, stolons and tubers) — asexual.

  • Annual weeds spread by seeds.
  • Perennials by seeds and by vegetative parts with man's aid.
  • Spread of perennial seeds aided by cultivation implements chopping and dragging vegetative parts into areas not previously infested.

7.1 Natural Means

This involves dissemination by wind, water, wild animals and forceful dehiscence (shattering) of pods or capsules containing seeds.

(i) Wind

  • Most common natural means of dispersal.
  • Seeds are tiny and light.
  • Some have "parachutes" to improve aerodynamics.
  • Others have wings and tufts of hair.
  • Such appendages are not part of the fruit which encases the seed. The fruit plus its appendages enclosing the seed make up the seed dispersal unit.

Examples:

  • Dandelion (Taraxacum officinale) — has parachute
  • Prickly lettuce (Lactuca serriola) — has tuft of hair at one end
  • Winged fruit of maple (Acer spp.)
  • Winged fruit of pine (Pinus spp.)
  • Hairy seed of cottonwood (Populus spp.)
  • Hooked fruit of cocklebur (Xanthium spp.)
  • Fruits of ground cherry (Physalis peruviana) with membranous envelope

(ii) Flowing Water

  • Flowing water is capable of transporting any type of seeds depending on volume and swiftness.
  • Seeds are introduced into water by sheet or wind erosion.
  • Rivers, streams, canals, ditches, furrows and uncontrolled flooding disseminate weeds.
  • Usually light, buoyant seeds or seed dispersal units are carried furthest.
  • Adaptations to water dispersal include: "corky" structure; air-filled "bladders" or pods.

Examples:

  • Seeds of carrot family (Umbelliferae)
  • Certain members of gourd family (Cucurbitaceae)
  • Datura stramonium (thorn apple)
  • Ground cherry (Physalis peruviana)

(iii) Wild Animals

Seeds dispersed by animals have the following characteristics:

  • Equipped with barbs, bristles, hooks and hairs and stick on the animal's hair or fur.

Examples:

  • Bidens pilosa — black jack
  • Common cocklebur (Xanthium pensylvanicum)
  • Setaria verticillata — love grass, bristly foxtail
  • Achyranthes aspera — devil's horse whip
  • Have sticky or mucilaginous seed coats which adhere to passing animals.

Examples (mucilaginous seeds):

  • Plantains (Plantago spp.)
  • Small mistletoe (Arceuthobium spp.)
  • Wild animals also carry viable seeds from place to place in their digestive tract, excreting the seed in their faeces during grazing or along migratory trails.

Examples:

  • Mesquite (Prosopis spp.)
  • Birds roosting along fence lines excrete viable seeds in their droppings. May also fly long distances with seed attached on feathers or in digestive system.
  • Birds and animals drop viable weed seeds plus manure (e.g. Lantana camara).
  • Other animals and insects may carry weed seeds for short distances e.g. dung beetle transporting fresh animal dung contaminated with weed seeds.

(iv) Forceful Dehiscence

Involves weeds whose seeds are encased in pods or capsules. They disperse seeds by ejecting or propelling the seed to distances of up to 6 m.

Examples:

  • Wood sorrel (Oxalis spp.)
  • Squirting cucumber (Ecballium elaterium)

7.2 Artificial Means

Associated with the movement of man and his agricultural material from place to place. Also involves man's cultural practices of arable (cultivated) lands.

Examples:

  1. Excavation and transportation of soil during road construction.
  2. Transport of building materials, including sand.
  3. Deliberate introduction as ornamentals.
  4. By farm machinery during field operations (ploughing, combine harvester) from field to field.
  5. Crop plants/seeds contaminated with weed seeds (principal means). Note seed mimicry.
  6. Weed seeds carried on cuffs (turn-ups) of trousers or attached to clothes.
  7. Accidental spread of waste from cleaning of crop seed e.g. during winnowing.
  8. Contamination in hay, silage and other animal feeding stuffs.
  9. Contaminants in straw and mulch.
  10. Contaminants in packaging materials (e.g. use of seeded grass linings in mango cartons).
  11. Domestic animals internally or externally while grazing, even mud in their hooves.

8. Methods of Weed Control

8.1 Definitions

Weed Control: Encompasses those practices whereby weed infestations are reduced below economic injury level. Weeds are not necessarily eliminated. Live with them but exert a seasonable check. The degree of control (ranging from poor to excellent) is dependent on the characteristics of the weed(s) involved and the effectiveness of the method(s) used.

To achieve better weed control the following information about weeds should be known:

  • Growth habit of the weed (life cycle, propagation and dissemination methods).
  • Habitat (where it thrives best) so that you can change the habitat.
  • Distribution (magnitude of the problem).

The amount of control is usually balanced between the cost involved and the amount of possible injury to the crop (yield loss). Control is the usual aim when dealing with annual weeds competing with farm crops.

Weed Eradication: Complete elimination of all live plants, plant parts, and seeds from an area. Weeds are usually established but have to be exterminated.

This is usually difficult because:

  • Weeds have microscopic seeds e.g. Striga.
  • Weeds exhibit prolonged dormancy in the soil e.g. Striga up to 20 years.
  • Weeds with natural mechanisms of spread.
  • Weed/crop seed mimicry (resemblance at the seed level).

8.2 Weed Control Techniques

  1. Preventive
  2. Cultural
  3. Mechanical (physical)
  4. Biological
  5. Chemical
  6. Integrated Weed Management (IWM)

8.3 Preventive Weed Control

Definition: Concerned with measures to prevent the introduction, establishment, and/or spread of specific weed species in areas not currently infested with these plant species.

People are the key to success of preventive weed control. Preventive practices include:

  • Using weed-free seeds (certified).
  • Use of weed-free manure and hay (compost manure and uproot weeds before seeding and feed to livestock before cutting hay).
  • Clean harvesting equipment (clean equipment between farms).
  • Elimination of weed infestations in or near irrigation waters and cultivated fields.

Preventive Weed Control Programs

  • (a) Regulatory (law): Involves inspection and application of agricultural act to quarantine weed-contaminated produce. Implementing authority is Kenya Plant Health Inspectorate Services (KEPHIS). Challenges: unauthorised routes (panya), corruption among implementing authorities, microscopic weed seeds, lack of qualified personnel to detect weed seeds, weed/crop mimicry, some weed seeds come with un-inspected food aid because of urgency.
  • (b) Research: Through basic or applied research and literature review. Identify new weeds, their biology e.g. propagation, uses, habitat, reproduction, dissemination and effective, cheap, integrated methods of control.
  • (c) Education: Educate farmers through agricultural extension with other stakeholders. Identification of noxious weeds (cause maximum damage, difficult to control and spread fast). How weeds propagate and spread and means of stopping them. Damage caused by weeds and possible economic uses. Integrated weed control practices.

8.4 Mechanical / Physical Weed Control

The physical methods of weed control involve the use of energy and include the following practices:

  • (a) Hand pulling (rouging)
  • (b) Hand hoeing
  • (c) Mowing
  • (d) Water management (flooding)
  • (e) Mulching (dead)
  • (f) Burning / flaming
  • (g) Machine tillage

(a) Hand Pulling

  • Effective for weed seedlings.
  • Effective for young established annual and biennial weed species.
  • Not effective in the control of established perennial weeds — underground perennating organs are not affected.
  • Labour intensive.
  • Adapted to small gardens.
  • For removal of weeds near or between crop plants where hoe cannot reach.

(b) Hoeing

  • Labour intensive.
  • One of the principal tools for weed control among small-scale farmers.
  • Effective for shallow-rooting weeds.
  • Effective for annual and biennial weed species.
  • Good for small-scale farmers with limited resources and abundant family hand labour.
  • It also stirs the soil leading to aeration.
  • Some closely planted (seeded) broadcast crops are difficult to hand weed without damage to crop.
  • Being replaced by labour-saving innovations because labour is scarce and expensive and farming is not attractive.

(c) Mowing

  • Removes top growth (apical dominance — removal of terminal buds removes auxins that depress lateral growth).
  • Repeated mowing prevents seed production and also starves underground parts.
  • The best time to mow is when underground root reserves are at the lowest level.
  • Removes apical dominance and repeated cuttings hasten food depletion and death of plants.
  • Effective on tall erect growing weeds.
  • It favours short growing plants by removing competition from tall plants (shadowing).
  • Most commonly used in pastures, along roadsides, in waste areas and to clear bushland prior to primary cultivation.
  • Pasturing and grazing of cattle, sheep or goats continuously are equivalent to mowing.

(d) Flooding

  • Effective only when the roots and/or shoots of the weeds are completely covered by water for a sufficiently long period.
  • Use is limited by soil type (one that allows ponding of water) e.g. heavy clays.
  • Subject to availability of sufficient water to provide flooding to desired depth.
  • Limited to certain crops such as paddy rice and arrow roots.
  • Limited to slope of land which is flat to collect water.
  • Effective on terrestrial weeds; encourages aquatic weeds, which are controlled by draining.
  • Good on certain perennial weeds.
  • Flooding kills weeds by: preventing root absorption of oxygen from the soil; when shoots are covered, preventing leaf absorption of carbon dioxide; due to turbidity of flood water, light intensity reaching flooded weeds is limiting for maximum photosynthesis.
  • Weed seeds should not be allowed to form in order to be disseminated by flood water.

(e) Mulching

  • Use of non-living organic and inorganic materials.
  • Materials used include plant parts, hay, manure, grass clippings, straw, sawdust, wood chips, rice hulls, coffee husks, paper and plastic film.
  • Objective is to completely exclude light from growing weed plants, thereby preventing photosynthesis and further growth.
  • Black paper and black plastic film (polythene of a specific gauge) have been used successfully in various horticultural and agronomic crops e.g. pineapple — Kenya Canners (Delmonte) in Thika, Kenya.
  • Note the cost of such materials; therefore only used in high-value crops.
  • Mulching materials should be free from weed seeds if an organic material is used.
  • Use cheap and readily available by-products to reduce cost of transportation.
  • Costs involved include cutting, transportation and spreading.
  • Ensure that the mulch material is not allelopathic to crops in which it is applied.

(f) Burning and Flaming

Burning:

  • Non-selective and destroys all vegetation.
  • Used for many years as a means of general weed control in non-cropped areas e.g. railroads, irrigation canals, drainage ditches and roadsides.
  • Repeated burning destroys annuals and can lead to predominance of perennial weed species, whose perennating organs under the soil are not affected.
  • Allows more soil erosion on slopes.
  • Destroys organic matter and other beneficial soil microbes.
  • Often destroys many weed seeds, but can stimulate germination of certain weed seeds.
  • Has been successfully used in the control of dodder (Cuscuta spp.).

Flaming:

  • Selective weed control.
  • Practical application for selective weed control in croplands began in the early 1940s.
  • Flame cultivator is a variation of the mechanical cultivator. Generally tractor-mounted; consists of fuel tank, pipelines, vaporizer, pressure regulator and burners. Fuel is butane or propane or a mixture under pressure.
  • Recent increase in petroleum price has reduced economic attractiveness.
  • Selective flaming operates on principles: crop plants are tall enough at time of flaming; flame directed towards ground near base of plants; flame will not strike leaves of crop plants or other tender parts; crop plants are woody and resistant to intense heat; weeds are small, succulent and susceptible to heat.
  • Used successfully in cotton, sugarcane and soybean.

Theory behind flaming: Heat intensity (fuel rate) and exposure time (forward speed) are synchronised so as to cause cell sap to expand, resulting in rupture of cell walls, but not actual combustion. Heat may also cause coagulation of cell protoplasm and denaturing of enzymes. Effect may not be apparent until several hours after application.

(g) Machine Tillage

  • Machine tillage or "cultivation" is and continues to be one of the principal means by which weeds are controlled.
  • Similar to hoeing except power for cultivating tools is provided by animals or mechanical engines rather than people.
  • Effective on annual, some biennial and perennial weeds.
  • Applicable in extensive areas where access is not limited by topography or weather.
  • If used systematically, can reduce weed seed supply in the soil.
  • Timely tillage operations are important in control of perennial weeds.
  • Ploughing controls perennial weeds by burying them and exposing their roots for desiccation.
  • Deep ploughing brings weed seeds up to soil surface and induces their germination, then they are destroyed before seeding.
  • Weed seeds that require light germinate when brought to soil surface; those that require darkness germinate when buried.
  • Harrowing and shallow cultivation also induces weed seeds to germinate due to improved aeration, then destroyed before seeding.
  • Take care not to injure roots or top parts of crop plants during weeding operations.

Perennial weeds may be controlled by tillage through:

  • Carbohydrate depletion: Top growth cut off; perennial plants draw upon root reserves to produce new top growth. Maximum depletion of root reserves occurs if cultivation is done 10–15 days after emergence of new growth. The period is longer if poor environmental conditions retard growth rate.
  • Desiccation: Desiccation of rhizomes, stolons, tubers, bulbs and roots. Leave them exposed on the soil surface. Requires repeated tillage. Ploughing operation is done early to allow for desiccation before planting. At best reduces weed population to tolerable levels.

Special Tillage Practices

  • (a) Delayed Tillage: Prepare seedbed → wait until weeds have germinated → till or harrow → seed the crop.
  • (b) Blind Tillage: Plant the crop → perform shallow tillage before the crop has emerged. Most practical when weeds emerge well ahead of crop.

8.5 Cultural Weed Control

Includes any husbandry or management practice that enhances a crop's ability to compete with weeds.

(a) Crop Interference

Involves selection of crops and agronomic practices that cancel the competitive ability of weeds.

  • (i) Competition: Select competitive crops and cultivars or varieties within the crop. Varietal difference in competitive ability exists e.g. spreading are better than erect varieties. Competitive varieties establish fast with dense canopy that smothers weeds.
  • (ii) Allelopathy: Select crops with allelopathic effects on weeds.
  • (iii) Plant population: Manipulation of planting arrangement and density but within recommended plant population.
  • (iv) Intercropping: Practice mixed cropping. Note crop compatibility. Common among small-scale farmers with limited land. Select plants with dissimilar growth habits e.g. broadleaf and grasses; fibrous and tap rooting; shade-tolerant intercrop e.g. cowpea and sorghum, groundnut/beans and maize.
  • (v) Crop rotation: Grow different taxonomic plant species on the same plot in consecutive seasons. Breaks weed cycle of weeds associated with certain crops. Enables rotation of herbicides in rotation crops to control weeds tolerant to previous one. Also controls insect pests and diseases. Improves soil fertility particularly nitrogen-fixing legumes; changes habitats of certain weeds e.g. high nitrogen controls parasitic Striga weed.
  • (vi) Smother crops: Crops highly competitive with weeds for growth factors.
    • Small and large seeded grains e.g. barley, ensilage maize, millet, rye, sorghum
    • Legumes e.g. alfalfa (lucerne), clovers, spreading cowpeas, sweet clover
    • Grasses e.g. Sudan grass, crested wheat grass
    • Others e.g. sunflower, Sesbania, buckwheat
    Don't use crops that may become weeds. Smother crop may be grown in rotation with less competitive crops.
  • (vii) Fertilizer placement: Hole and furrow placement and not broadcast. Gives the crop a head start in competition.
  • (viii) Timing planting: Early planting for the crop to benefit from N-flush or Birch Effect. Gives the crop a head start in competition.
  • (ix) Liming: Changes the habitat for the weed. Create good pH for the crop but not the weed. Example: dandelion (Taraxacum officinale) is checked by increasing soil acidity.
  • (x) Irrigation and Drainage: Use discriminatory irrigation method that supplies the crop with water but not the weed. Example: drip irrigation. Drainage controls aquatic weeds and encourages terrestrial weeds.

8.6 Chemical Weed Control

Chemical weed control functions on the basis that certain chemicals are capable of killing some kinds of plants without significant injury to others. Any chemical agent that kills or greatly inhibits plant growth is known as a herbicide. The use of chemicals for weed control has been practiced to some extent since about 1900.

Definitions

  • (a) Contact herbicide: A herbicide that is phytotoxic by contact with plant tissue e.g. paraquat (Gramoxone). Proper plant coverage with spray mixture is important for effective weed control. Dense canopy and tall weeds may shield others.
  • (b) Growth regulator: A substance used for controlling or modifying plant growth processes without appreciable phytotoxic effect at the dosage applied. Some herbicides act as growth regulators when applied in sub-lethal dose e.g. 2,4-D and MCPA. Such herbicides exhibit distorted growth symptoms (epinasty).
  • (c) Non-selective herbicide: A chemical that is generally toxic to plants without regard to species e.g. paraquat (Gramoxone); glyphosate (Roundup).
  • (d) Selective herbicide: A herbicide that is more toxic to some plant species than to others e.g. 2,4-D in maize, oryzalin in rice, metribuzin in Irish potatoes, pendimethalin in maize/bean intercrop and vegetables. Selectivity is a function of dosage and method of application.
  • (e) Post-emergence herbicides: Applied after emergence of the specified weed or crop e.g. 2,4-D; paraquat (Gramoxone); glyphosate (Roundup).
  • (f) Pre-emergence herbicides: Applied prior to emergence of the specified weed or crop e.g. atrazine, alachlor, pendimethalin, lasso/atrazine.
  • (g) Pre-emergence incorporated: Applied after seeding and incorporated in the soil above the seed because they are volatile and would be lost e.g. trifluralin.

Advantages of Using Herbicides

  • Speed of control is high and can be used on extensive areas.
  • Less laborious than manual control.
  • Control in critical period when weather doesn't allow other methods.
  • Selective control is achieved.
  • Possible economy (requires proof by research).
  • Control in closely spaced crops is possible where hoes cannot reach.
  • Control of special weed problems e.g. where Kikuyu grass (Pennisetum clandestinum) is a problem apply glyphosate (Roundup) which is systemic.
  • Mixtures permit broad-spectrum control for both broadleaf and grass weeds.
  • Reduced erosion when used to control weeds in hilly areas.
  • Applied soon after sowing/planting to achieve early-season weed control before critical period.
  • Avoids destruction effects (e.g. injury to crop roots) by mechanical means e.g. in coffee where feeder roots are close to the soil surface.

Disadvantages of Using Herbicides

  • Special equipment required.
  • May fail to kill the weeds if not properly used.
  • Certain technical skill required in handling and application.
  • Potential for crop injury both within and outside target area (note drift).
  • Selectivity may be a problem under intercropping systems.
  • Weeds may develop resistance if same herbicide used year after year.
  • Not totally safe for animals and humans due to poisoning.
  • Potential for environmental contamination.
  • May create reliance on an imported product.
  • Secondary weeds may become a primary problem after they are selectively controlled.
  • Cost: limited purchasing power and credit make it difficult for small-scale farmers to buy herbicides.

8.7 Biological Weed Control

Definition: Involves the utilisation of natural enemies for the control of certain weeds. It does not control all weeds at the same time.

Natural enemies used to control weeds may include:

  • Insects that oviposit and feed on weeds
  • Plant diseases caused by pathogens
  • Certain other plants with allelopathic effects
  • Herbivorous fish, freshwater snails
  • Selective grazing by livestock (sheep and goats)
  • Ducks and geese eat tubers of Cyperus spp.

Objective of Biological Control

Reduction and regulation of weed population below the level of economic injury, and not to eradicate the weeds. May be achieved through direct or indirect action of bio-agent.

  • Direct action: Boring into the plant and weakening its structure so that it collapses; consumption or destruction of "vital" plant parts e.g. vascular bundles (xylem and phloem), leaves, flowers.
  • Indirect action: The biotic agent cancels the competitive advantage of the weed by reducing its vigour of growth and reproduction ability; enhancement of conditions favourable for weed pathogens.

General Principles

  1. Ensure that the agent will not attack any plant other than those for which control is desired, by conducting starvation tests.
  2. Obtain bio-agent from areas climatically similar to area of introduction for survival.
  3. Reduce competition between two or more bio-agents introduced by selecting species with different feeding habits e.g. insects that attack different plant parts — roots, leaves, flowers, stems or seeds; others are gall-formers such as nematodes.
  4. Introduce several bio-agents with different feeding habits to speed up control of one weed.
  5. Natural enemies, in general, are sought in the natural habitat of the weed.
  6. Natural enemies may also be found outside the weed's natural habitat but as natural enemies of related plants.

Starvation Test Procedure

  • Done in a screen house in a restrictive environment where the bio-agent cannot escape.
  • All plants useful to man are exposed to bio-agent except the weed for which control is desired.
  • Include plants/crops that are in a botanical class with the weed to be controlled.
  • Aim: test whether the bio-agent would rather die than feed on available alternative plants.
  • Bio-agent, if an insect, oviposits and completes its life cycle on the weed it is to selectively control and not on any other.
  • If bio-agent passes this test then it is released in cages to evaluate effectiveness in controlling target weed in its habitat.
  • If effective then it is multiplied and released for weed control.

Classification of Insects by Feeding Habits

  • Monophagous: Feed only on one plant species.
  • Oligophagous: Feed on a few plant species.
  • Polyphagous: Feed on several plant species.

Preference of phytophagous insects for particular food plants is largely influenced by presence of phagostimulants (feeding stimulants). Other factors: nutritional balance, surface hairiness (pubescence), other physical conditions. Botanical groups usually have characteristic phagostimulants; similar phagostimulants may be present in botanically unrelated plants.

Weed Control Using Fungi as Bio-Control Agents

Fungi are the principal microbial biotic agents used in the most recent work to suppress weeds.

Factors contributing to popularity of fungi as bio-agents:

  • Organisms are ubiquitous (abundant).
  • Highly host specific.
  • Destructive to the host plant.
  • Persistent.
  • Can be readily produced in-vitro.

Approaches in Use of Fungi

  • (i) Classical Approach: Initial inoculation of the weed with self-sustaining endemic or foreign pathogenic fungi. Suited to conditions where annual application is not economical. Suited in expansive areas such as perennial weed control in aquatic environment, pasture land, rangeland, wastelands. Persistence and efficient natural dispersal of pathogen are vital (as demonstrated by rust fungi). Example: suppression of curly dock (Rumex crispus) in Europe with a fungal rust (Uromyces rumicis).
  • (ii) Mycoherbicide Approach: Annual application of endemic or foreign pathogenic fungi in a manner similar to herbicides. Fungi produce chemical toxicants which are herbicidal. Better suited to control annual weeds in cultivated croplands where rapid control is desirable. Example: use of isolate of fungus Alternaria macrospora in control of spurred anoda (Anoda cristata). Granular formulation (spores + mycelia + vermiculite) applied pre- or post-emergence controlled the weed in greenhouse and field tests.

Commercial Mycoherbicides

  • Devine (Trade name): Active ingredient Phytophthora palmivora that causes fungal disease. Non-toxic to mammals. Successfully used for control of milk weed vine (Morrenia odorata) in Florida citrus plantations. Formulated as spores of naturally occurring fungus.
  • Collego (Trade name): Active ingredient living spores of fungus Colletotrichum gloeosporioides spp. aeschynomene. Formulated as 15% concentrate of viable spores. Used for control of northern joint vetch or curly indigo (Aeschynomene virginica) in rice and soybean.

Other Examples of Successful Biological Weed Control

  • Control of prickly pear (Opuntia stricta) infested 25 million hectares in Australia — controlled by Cactoblastis cactorum, a phytophagous insect.
  • Lantana (Lantana camara) controlled in Hawaii by leaf-eating insect Teleonemia scrupulosa in conjunction with 3 other leaf-feeding Lepidoptera.
  • Skeleton weed (Chondrilla juncea) spread over 25 million ha in Australia — good control achieved with Puccinia chondrillina (a fungus).

Advantages of Biological Weed Control

  1. No chemical residues or toxic hazard.
  2. Host specific.
  3. Self-perpetuating once established (classical approach).
  4. Less chance of weed developing resistance.
  5. Energy efficient (bio-agent feeds directly on primary source of energy, the weed).
  6. Effective in inaccessible areas.
  7. Inexpensive after initial cost of development.
  8. No pollution of environment.

Disadvantages of Biological Weed Control

  1. More useful in single selected species of weeds.
  2. One weed controlled, others take over.
  3. Irreversible after release.
  4. Effective more on perennials.
  5. Good in large areas e.g. rangeland or water body.
  6. Slow compared to fast action of herbicides.
  7. May attack crops after wiping out its food sources.
  8. Adaptability of bio-agent to tropical environment may be a problem.
  9. May have predators where introduced.
  10. Use of limited foreign exchange if bio-agent is imported.

9. Herbicide Identification & Classification

9.1 Four Possible Identities of Herbicides

  1. Chemical formula: Basic rules of chemical nomenclature are followed.
  2. Common name: Begins with a small letter; assigned by organisations such as the British Standards Institution or the Weed Science Society of America.
  3. Trade name: Begins with a capital letter; property of the manufacturer.
  4. Chemical structure: Shows arrangement of the elements from which the herbicide is structured.

Example 1

  • Chemical formula: 3-(3,4-dichlorophenyl)-1,1-dimethylurea
  • Common name: diuron
  • Trade names: Diurex, Karmex, Telvar

Example 2

  • Chemical formula: (2,4-dichlorophenyl) acetic acid
  • Common name: 2,4-D
  • Trade names: Dacamine, Esteron, Formula 40, Hendonal, Trinoxal

Example 3

  • Chemical formula: N-(phosphonomethyl) glycine
  • Common name: glyphosate
  • Trade name: Round-up

(2) Selective vs Non-Selective

Selectivity is relative and depends on the herbicide rate and the state of plant growth.

(3) Foliage Acting and Soil Acting

Some herbicides fall in only one category. Some are effective on both leaves and soils.

(4) Contact and Translocated

Translocated are also systemic.

(5) Mode of Action

Herbicides interfere with one or more of the physiological processes of a plant e.g.:

  • Acting as mitotic poison
  • Regulating growth
  • Inhibiting shoots of germinating seedlings
  • Inhibiting roots of germinating seedlings
  • Inhibiting photosynthesis
  • Increasing cell wall permeability
  • Inhibiting oxidative phosphorylation
  • Inhibiting protein synthesis and/or metabolism

(6) Classification by Use

  • Foliage applied: Contact (bromoxynil), propanil, translocated (2,4-D), dalapon, selective
  • Soil applied: Foliage applied, mobile in plant (atrazine), pre-emergence (PE), non-mobile (trifluralin), pre-plant incorporated (PPI)
  • Contact: Paraquat, diquat
  • Translocated: Glyphosate
  • Systemic: Non-selective
  • Soil applied (temporary sterilants): Fumigant (metham sodium, methyl bromide), persistent sterilants, residual (sodium borate, simazine, TCA)

(7) Organic and Inorganic

Determined by the basic elements forming the herbicides.

(8) Classification by Herbicide Families

Based on chemical affinities or resemblance of chemical groups. Gives similarities to their activities on weeds.

A. Inorganic Herbicides

They were the first ones to be used for weed control. Romans used ashes and brine for vegetation control as early as biblical times. The salts have been used for many years, are highly soluble in water and act as soil sterilants.

Characteristics of inorganic herbicides:

  • Applied in large amounts, some up to 900 kg/ha; therefore storage and transportation is expensive.
  • Bulky to handle.
  • Composed of simple elements such as Na, Cl, Boron, K in their structure.
  • Persistent in the soil.
  • Applied as water solutions or granules.
  • Highly soluble in water.
  • Can catch fire spontaneously when in contact with organic material e.g. cotton.

B. Organic Herbicides

Characteristics:

  • Have C, H and O and other elements e.g. N, S, P and halogens (Fl, Cl, I).
  • Carbon presence enables it to bond and form chains; chains can be continuous or closed.
  • Applied in small quantities such as 0.3 kg/ha (metribuzin in Irish potatoes).
  • Require small space for storage and are cheap to transport.
  • Not persistent; life span equivalent to life cycle of the crop.
  • Do not catch fire spontaneously but some are volatile and should not be opened near a naked flame; shown on the label.

(1) Organic Non-Nitrogenous Herbicides

  • (a) Oils: Diesel, paraffinic additives and xylene type of aromatic oils.
  • (b) Substituted phenols: Pentachlorophenol (PCP).
  • (c) Phenylacetic acid: Fenac and chlorfenac.
  • (d) Halogenated aliphatic acids: Trichloroacetic acid and dalapon.
  • (e) Phenoxyaliphatic acids: Series of compounds in which the phenoxy-nucleus is linked with acetic, propionic and butyric acids. Straight chains (aliphatic). Soluble in water. Have a hormone-type of action on broadleaf weeds. Injury symptoms include epinasty (distorted growth). Include MCPA, MCPB, 2,4-D, 2,4-DB and 2,4,5-T (banned due to production of dioxide which depletes ozone layer). Have growth regulating properties. Kill broadleaf weeds only. Systemic through transpiration and photosynthetic streams. Both soil and foliar applied. Tend to accumulate in meristematic tissue. Low mammalian toxicity. Cheap and readily available.
  • (f) Aromatic acids (Benzoic acid herbicides): 2,3,6-TBA, dicamba, chloramben, dacthal. Similar growth regulating properties like phenoxy-type herbicides. Low mammalian toxicity. Short soil persistence except for 2,3,6-TBA. Have activity on both grasses and broadleaf weeds.

(2) Organic Nitrogenous Herbicides

  • (a) Amides: Diphenamid (good in tomatoes) and chlorthiamid. Act on annual grasses. Cause severe stunting of shoots. Low mammalian toxicity. Somewhat persistent in soil.
  • (b) Anilides: Propachlor, propanil, alachlor, butachlor, metolachlor, perfluidone. Vary a great deal in action and weed species controlled. Propanil is a good post-emergent herbicide in rice. Some act by pre-controlling germinating annual weeds especially grasses. Perfluidone has activity in retarding Cyperus rotundus underground buds. Low mammalian toxicity. Alachlor used in controlling aquatic weeds but can kill fish. Average soil persistence. Used in several crops including maize, beans and their intercrops.
  • (c) Nitrophenyl ethers or Diphenyl ethers: Nitrofen, fluorodifen, oxyfluorfen, acifluorfen, bifenox. Have two benzene rings hence diphenyl ethers. Soil and foliar applied. Persist in soil. Oxyfluorfen can be applied as post-emergent and has soil residual activity in coffee crop. Inhibit respiration and photosynthesis. Low mammalian toxicity. Vary in activity on weeds.
  • (d) Nitroanilines: Trifluralin, nitralin, benfluralin, pendimethalin, oryzalin. Have a common dinitroaniline nucleus. Also called dinitroaniline herbicides. Mostly soil-applied. Good for annual weeds. Also affect underground buds and stolons of perennial weeds. Many are soil incorporated e.g. trifluralin (volatile). Pendimethalin good for maize–beans intercrop. Inhibit all cell divisions and cause stunting. Low mammalian toxicity. Average soil persistence depending on dosage.
  • (e) Ureas: Dimon, fenuron, monuron, neburon, fluometuron, linuron, diuron. Have urea derivatives and are active through the soil. Kill many annual weeds. Some products can be sprayed post-emergence. Inhibit photosynthesis. Low mammalian toxicity. Average to long persistence.
  • (f) Thiocarbamate herbicides: EPTC, butylate, cycloate, diallate, pebulate, triallate, vernolate, benthiocarb. Soil acting and need incorporation because of high volatility. Uptake through roots and shoots. Retards growth of shoots, buds and roots of weeds. For wild oat control use diallate or triallate. Diallate and triallate persist for many months. Low mammalian toxicity.
  • (g) Carbamates: Asulam, barban, chlorpropham, propham, phenmedipham (Dacthal), carbaryl. Act by inhibiting acetylcholinesterase. Used as pre- and post-emergence herbicides. Low to moderate persistence. Examples: asulam (selective on broadleaves in rice), barban (aquatic weeds), chlorpropham (pre-emergence in potatoes).

(3) Other Organic Herbicide Groups

  • Organophosphates: e.g. malathion (mostly insecticides, limited herbicidal use).
  • Benzobicyclon: New chemistry for graminicides; inhibits cellulose synthesis.
  • HPPD-inhibitors: e.g. mesotrione, sulcotrione, norflurzon.
